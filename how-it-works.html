How MacBreeze Works

Last updated: September 9, 2026

MacBreeze changes how your Mac’s fans behave, and to do that it installs a small privileged component. That is a reasonable thing to be cautious about, so this page describes exactly what it reads, what it changes, what it can’t do, and what leaves your Mac. If anything here is unclear, ask us before you buy.

What MacBreeze reads

MacBreeze reads sensors your Mac already exposes through its System Management Controller:

  • Temperatures — processor, graphics, and battery
  • Fan speeds, in RPM, and each fan’s minimum and maximum
  • Power state — whether you are on battery or mains, and the charging wattage

It reads nothing else. It does not see your files, your screen, your keystrokes, your network traffic, or which applications you use — with one narrow exception: if you create an app automation, MacBreeze checks whether that one specific application is running, so it can apply the rule you asked for.

What MacBreeze changes

Fan speed. That is the whole list.

It does not touch processor frequency, voltage, power limits, or any other part of how your Mac runs. It cannot make your Mac faster, and it cannot make it draw more power than Apple allows. It sets a target speed for fans that already exist, between the minimum and maximum your own hardware reports.

You can set that target directly as a percentage or an RPM figure, or draw a curve that ties fan speed to temperature. Handing control back to macOS is always one click away.

The fan helper, and why it exists

Talking to the System Management Controller requires privileges a normal application does not have. This is also why MacBreeze is not on the Mac App Store: App Store applications run in a sandbox that forbids exactly this kind of hardware access.

So MacBreeze ships a separate, very small program — the fan helper — which lives inside the MacBreeze app bundle and is installed through macOS’s own approval mechanism. You are asked for permission; it cannot install itself quietly.

The helper accepts exactly six instructions, and nothing else:

  • report its version, so MacBreeze can refuse to talk to a mismatched helper
  • report a snapshot of the current sensor readings
  • set a fan’s mode and target speed
  • receive a heartbeat saying MacBreeze is still alive
  • report what this particular Mac’s hardware supports
  • update the temperature at which the overheat safety net triggers

There is no instruction that runs a command, reads a file, or opens a network connection, because no such instruction exists in it. The helper never connects to the internet.

It also refuses to talk to anything but MacBreeze itself. Before accepting a connection it verifies the calling program’s code signature — signed by Apple, carrying MacBreeze’s bundle identifier, under our developer team. Another program on your Mac cannot drive your fans by pretending to be MacBreeze.

Three safety nets

Setting fan speeds by hand carries an obvious risk: fans set too low during heavy work. Three independent protections address it, and two of them run inside the helper rather than the app, so they keep working even if MacBreeze itself stops.

  • Auto-Blast. If temperature crosses a threshold, the helper drives the fans to full speed on its own, regardless of what you set. The threshold can be moved between 70 °C and 95 °C, and no further in either direction.
  • The 45-second heartbeat. MacBreeze tells the helper it is still running. If that stops — the app quit, crashed, or hung — the helper returns your fans to normal macOS control by itself after 45 seconds. A crashed application cannot leave your fans stuck.
  • Your Mac’s own firmware. Apple’s thermal protection sits underneath all of this and is not modified, disabled, or bypassed. If your Mac decides it must slow down or shut down to protect itself, it still will.

What leaves your Mac

MacBreeze contacts two addresses, both ours, and nothing else:

  • license.macbreeze.app — to start your trial, check your licence, and manage which Macs it is active on.
  • macbreeze.app — to ask whether a newer version exists, and to download it if you agree to update.

A licence check sends your licence key, a hashed device identifier, and a short device label such as “MacBook Pro · M4 Pro”. The label deliberately is not your computer’s name, which usually contains a person’s real name.

The device identifier is your Mac’s hardware UUID, hashed with SHA-256 on your Mac before it is sent. The original never leaves the machine and we never receive it, so it cannot be reconstructed from anything we store.

There is no analytics, no telemetry, no crash reporting and no advertising identifier in MacBreeze. We cannot see your temperatures, your fan settings, your profiles, or how often you use the app. Those never leave your Mac.

Where your settings live

Your profiles, automations, curves and preferences are stored on your own Mac in the standard location for application settings. Your licence key is kept in the macOS Keychain.

You can export your settings to a file to move them to another Mac. That file deliberately contains no licence key and no information about your machine, so it is safe to send to yourself however you like.

Updates

MacBreeze checks for updates and asks before installing one. You can turn automatic checking off in Settings.

Every update is signed twice: with our Apple Developer ID and notarised by Apple, and separately with our own update-signing key. MacBreeze refuses any update whose signature does not match, so a tampered download cannot be installed even if someone managed to serve you one.

What MacBreeze cannot do

  • It cannot add fan control to a Mac that has no controllable fans. On fanless models — the MacBook Air among them — MacBreeze shows temperatures and nothing else, and says so.
  • It cannot exceed your fans’ real maximum speed, or drop below their real minimum.
  • It cannot cool a Mac beyond what its existing hardware is physically capable of.
  • Without the helper installed and approved, it cannot control fans at all — it will tell you so rather than pretend to work.

Removing it

Turn off fan control in MacBreeze’s settings and the helper is unregistered and removed. Then drag MacBreeze to the Bin like any other application. Your fans return to normal macOS control immediately, and nothing is left behind that keeps running.

Free, trial and Pro

The download is free and needs no payment details. Every new installation starts a 14-day trial with all Pro features unlocked, with no card required.

When the trial ends, MacBreeze keeps working — it is not a demo that stops. Monitoring, manual fan control and the Auto-Blast safety net stay free permanently, along with two profiles and one app automation. Pro removes those limits and adds the visual curve editor, a custom overheat threshold, and overheat notifications.
